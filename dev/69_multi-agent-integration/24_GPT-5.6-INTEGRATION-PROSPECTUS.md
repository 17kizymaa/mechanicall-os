- What have you inferred?

Alias design

Your instinct about aliases is useful.

A project alias should be:

- memorable;
- non-sensitive;
- easy to say;
- stable over the project;
- not derived from private client information;
- distinct enough for logs and folders.

For example:

amber-window
quiet-orbit
paper-bridge
north-kite
small-harbour

Keep separate records mapping:

client identity ↔ project alias

Do not expose the identity map to agents unless required.

The alias helps the project become a coherent object without casually propagating a person’s name, contact information or sensitive subject through prompts, screenshots and repositories.

The “circle” growth model

Your circle metaphor is good if you treat growth as trust propagation, not merely reach.

Personal circle
→ project circle
→ referral circle
→ professional circle
→ institutional circle

Circle 1: people who already know you

This includes your existing personal contacts and Instagram followers.

Purpose:

- learn language;
- gain projects;
- obtain honest reactions;
- collect before/after stories;
- build trust in the process.

Do not use all 100 followers as one indiscriminate “bomb.” That can create noise without commitment.

Use staged exposure.

Wave 1: high-trust individuals

Privately invite 5–10 people who:

- know you personally;
- have visible unfinished digital projects;
- will tolerate an early process;
- will tell you when something is confusing.

Wave 2: personal public story

Share:

- why you built it;
- your mum story;
- one real, consented project transformation;
- a limited number of project places;
- a clear application link.

Wave 3: follow-up proof

A few days later, share:

- what kinds of projects arrived;
- one thing you learned;
- one completed outcome;
- the next limited intake window.

That is stronger than one launch post followed by silence.

Circle 2: people adjacent to completed projects

Every successful project should create a natural question:

Who else has a similar mess?

Ask the client—not aggressively—whether someone they know might benefit.

Referral copy:

“I’m taking a few more projects like this. If someone comes to mind, you can send them this page. There’s no obligation to participate.”

A referral from a completed project is more valuable than a hundred low-context impressions.

Circle 3: specialized communities

Do not promote “Mechanicall” everywhere in the abstract.

Enter communities with a specific use case:

- indie creators with unfinished sites;
- artists with disorganized portfolios;
- small organizations with scattered digital materials;
- students with research/project overload;
- local businesses with neglected web presences;
- AI users overwhelmed by generated material.

Lead with:

“I’m helping people turn this particular kind of digital mess into a finished transition.”

Not:

“I have invented a human-authority protocol.”

The philosophy belongs behind the service promise.

Circle 4: high-leverage individuals

A “big individual” is valuable if they possess one or more of:

- an interesting project;
- a trusted audience;
- recurring project needs;
- institutional access;
- capacity to pay;
- ability to refer similar people.

Do not optimize purely for follower count.

A person with 500 trusted followers in a focused community may be much more valuable than someone with 100,000 generic followers.

The ideal high-leverage client produces:

strong outcome

+ credible endorsement

Circle 5: organizations

Organizations come after your workflow survives multiple individual projects.

They will require:

- predictable turnaround;
- data-handling policy;
- scope boundaries;
- pricing;
- identity and access controls;
- records of approval where consequences matter;
- repeatable output quality;
- ability to handle more than one stakeholder.

Do not rush there simply because “bigger circle” sounds better. Institutional work amplifies operational weaknesses.
